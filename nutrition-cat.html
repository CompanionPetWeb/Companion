The Full Reference

Everything your cat needs, in one place

If a food isn't listed here, play it safe and treat it as a "check with your vet first" — this covers the essentials, not every food out there.

Can my cat eat:
Protein Vitamin Mineral Carbohydrate Fiber Healthy Fat

Protein

The core of a cat's diet — builds muscle and supplies taurine

Muscle meats
ChickenSkinless, cooked plain — a cat diet staple
TurkeyLean and easy to digest, cooked plain
BeefLean cuts, cooked, fat trimmed off
RabbitLean, novel protein — good for sensitive cats
DuckCooked, skin removed — good for variety
Organ meats (feed regularly, in moderation)
HeartOne of the richest natural sources of taurine — cats need this daily
LiverRich in vitamin A — small amounts, ~5% of diet max
KidneyNutrient-dense, cooked, small portions
Seafood (occasional, not a staple)
Cooked fish (salmon, tuna)Deboned, plain — not the main diet
Sardines (in water)Small amounts — omega-3 plus taurine
Eggs & bones
Whole eggsFully cooked — scrambled or boiled, no salt
Raw chicken necksSized for a cat, supervised — natural teeth cleaning
Avoid
Raw fish as a regular dietContains thiaminase, destroys vitamin B1 over time
Excess liver/organ meatVitamin A builds up and can become toxic
Cooked bonesSplinter easily — choking/gut injury risk
Fish-only dietsMissing nutrients from muscle/organ meat
Cats are obligate carnivores — unlike dogs, they need taurine from animal tissue every day, since their bodies can't make enough on their own. A taurine-deficient diet can lead to serious heart disease over time.

Vitamin

Supports vision, immune health, and skin — mostly from animal sources

Animal-based sources (most important for cats)
Liver (small amounts)Preformed vitamin A — cats can't convert plant beta-carotene like dogs can
Egg yolkCooked — vitamin A, D, and B12
Fish / fish oilCooked — vitamin D and E
Small plant additions (occasional extras only)
Cooked pumpkinPlain puree, small amount
Cooked carrotsSmall amount, mashed — minor direct benefit
Avoid
Lily plants (all parts)Extremely toxic to cats specifically — can cause fatal kidney failure
Onions, garlic, chivesDamage red blood cells, even cooked
Grapes & raisinsBest avoided even though the risk is less proven in cats
Raw potato / green tomatoSolanine in unripe/green parts is toxic
Cats process vitamins differently than dogs — most notably, they can't efficiently turn plant-based beta-carotene into vitamin A, so animal sources like liver and egg yolk matter far more than vegetables do.
💠

Mineral

Strengthens bones and teeth, supports nerve and muscle function

Great sources
Sardines (with bone)Calcium, phosphorus, omega-3
Raw chicken necksNatural calcium and phosphorus, raw only
Eggshell (ground)A pinch — concentrated calcium
Avoid
Excess magnesium/mineral supplementsLinked to urinary crystal formation (FLUTD) in some cats
Calcium supplements without guidanceCan throw off the calcium-phosphorus balance
Mineral balance matters even more for cats than dogs because of urinary tract sensitivity — some cats are prone to crystals or blockages. Any supplementation should go through a vet.

Carbohydrate

Cats need very little — protein and fat are their main fuel

Acceptable in small amounts
Cooked white ricePlain, small amount — gentle if needed
Cooked pumpkinPlain puree — more for fiber than energy
Avoid
Grain-heavy diets as the main foodLinked to obesity and diabetes in cats
Bread & pastaRefined, little nutritional value for a cat
Corn on the cobChoking/blockage hazard
Unlike dogs, cats are obligate carnivores with almost no dietary need for carbohydrates. Their bodies are built to run on protein and fat.
🌿

Fiber

Supports digestion and helps manage hairballs

Great sources
Cooked pumpkinThe classic choice — helps stool quality and hairballs
Psyllium husk (vet-guided)Sometimes used for chronic hairball/constipation issues
Green beansLow-calorie, cooked or raw, plain
Avoid
Too much, too fastCan cause loose stool or gas
Corn cobsNot digestible — real blockage risk
Fiber is especially useful for cats prone to hairballs — pumpkin is the most common go-to, but introduce it gradually.

Healthy Fat

Supports skin, coat, and energy — cats need more fat than dogs relative to size

Great sources
Fish oil (vet-dosed)Omega-3 EPA/DHA — skin, coat, and joints
Sardines (in water)Omega-3 plus calcium
Chicken fat (rendered)Small amount — cats run on higher fat than dogs
Avoid
Fried or greasy foodHigh pancreatitis risk — cats can develop it too
Bacon grease / trimmingsVery high fat load at once
Macadamia & other nutsNot part of a cat's natural diet — avoid entirely
Cats naturally thrive on a higher proportion of fat than dogs do — still, portions should stay modest and any new fat source should be introduced slowly.
General guidance only — always confirm portions, routines, and health concerns with a veterinarian who knows your cat.
